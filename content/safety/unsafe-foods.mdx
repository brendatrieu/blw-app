Most foods are fine for baby-led weaning once they're cut and prepared the right way. A short list of foods, though, carry a choking risk that prep alone can't fully solve, or need specific handling to be safe. This is the reference to check when you're not sure.

## Foods to avoid completely under age 4–5

**Whole nuts and seeds** — a whole nut or large seed is roughly airway-sized, hard, and doesn't compress or dissolve. This includes peanuts, almonds, cashews, sunflower seeds, and pumpkin seeds served whole. Nut and seed *butters* are fine when prepared correctly (see below) — it's the whole, hard form that's the hazard, not the food itself.

Because of this, most pediatric guidance recommends holding off on whole nuts and seeds until somewhere between age 4 and 5, well past the baby-led weaning window. This is separate from the question of nut *allergies* — introducing peanut and tree nut as smooth, thinned purees or butters early is actually recommended for allergy prevention (see our [allergen introduction guide](/safety/allergen-introduction)). Whole nuts are a choking issue, not an allergy-timing issue.

## Foods that need specific cutting — not avoidance

A number of everyday foods are genuinely fine, but only in the right shape:

- **Grapes** — cut lengthwise into quarters, not just in half. A whole or halved grape is close to the exact size and shape to plug a small airway.
- **Cherry tomatoes** — same rule: quartered lengthwise, never left whole or simply halved.
- **Large blueberries** — cut lengthwise (in half or quarters depending on size); small blueberries can usually be smashed or served whole once your baby has some chewing skill, but larger ones should always be cut.
- **Hot dogs and sausages** — never serve coin-shaped rounds. If serving at all, cut lengthwise into thin strips and then into small pieces, never into a disc shape that matches an airway.
- **Hard raw vegetables and apple chunks** — raw carrot coins, hard apple chunks, and similar firm produce should be avoided in a hard, chunky form. Steam or cook vegetables until they're soft enough to squish easily between your fingers; serve apple cooked or grated rather than in raw chunks until your baby is confidently chewing (typically well past 12 months).

## Foods with a shape or texture that's inherently risky

- **Popcorn** — its irregular, light shape makes it easy to inhale into the airway; hulls can also lodge in the throat. Skip it for the baby-led weaning years.
- **Marshmallows** — soft on the outside but compress into a dense, airway-shaped plug and don't dissolve quickly. Avoid.
- **Nut butter globs** — a spoonful or thick smear of peanut or almond butter is sticky and can coat the roof of the mouth or throat. Instead, thin nut butters with warm water or breastmilk/formula until they're a runny, drippable consistency, and spread it thinly on toast strips or stir it into another food rather than offering it in a thick dollop or on a spoon.

## Choking risk isn't the only concern — some foods carry other hazards

- **High-mercury fish** (shark, swordfish, king mackerel, tilefish, bigeye tuna) — mercury accumulates in a developing nervous system, so these are best avoided entirely in early childhood. Lower-mercury options like salmon, sardines, and light canned tuna (in moderation) are great iron-rich choices instead.
- **Unpasteurized foods** — unpasteurized milk, juice, and soft cheeses can carry harmful bacteria that a baby's immune system is less equipped to handle. Check labels and stick to pasteurized versions of dairy and juice.
- **Raw or undercooked egg and meat** — cook eggs and all meat, poultry, and fish thoroughly for babies and young children; skip runny yolks, soft-scrambled-to-raw eggs, rare meat, and raw fish/sushi until your child is older and their immune system has matured.

## A quick mental model

When you're looking at a food and you're not sure, ask two questions: **Is it a whole, round, or coin shape close to the size of a small airway?** and **Is it hard, sticky, or fibrous enough that it won't easily compress or dissolve if it gets to the back of the throat?** If the answer to either is yes, change the shape (cut, quarter, mash, cook softer) before serving. This is the same logic behind our [safe eating setup guidance](/safety/gagging-vs-choking) — the goal isn't to avoid textures forever, it's to introduce them in a form your baby's current chewing skill can actually manage.

## When your baby is ready for more

As chewing skills mature (usually somewhere in the second year, and highly individual), many of these restrictions loosen — raw apple chunks, whole grapes, and firmer textures gradually become appropriate. This is a gradual transition based on your individual child's chewing ability, not a birthday-triggered switch. If you're ever unsure whether your child is ready for a firmer texture, it's always fine to wait a little longer or ask your pediatrician.
