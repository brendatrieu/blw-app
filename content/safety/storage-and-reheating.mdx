Baby-led weaning tends to generate a lot of prepped food — a batch of lentil fritters here, a puree there, a pot of rice you're portioning across the week. Getting the storage windows right protects your baby from foodborne illness without you having to guess or over-worry every time you open the fridge.

## The core rules, before the table

A few rules apply across almost everything:

- **Cool food before refrigerating or freezing it.** Don't put a hot pot straight into the fridge — let it cool at room temperature briefly first (but not for hours; see the room-temperature window below), or speed it up with an ice bath.
- **Reheat prepared food once, and only once.** Repeated reheating cycles give bacteria more opportunity to multiply between each cooling and reheating. Portion food into baby-sized servings *before* freezing or refrigerating, so you're only ever pulling out and reheating what you'll actually use.
- **Reheat until steaming hot all the way through, then cool to a safe eating temperature before serving** — this matters both for killing bacteria and for not burning your baby's mouth.
- **Anything your baby's saliva has touched gets discarded, not saved.** Once a spoon has been in your baby's mouth and back in a bowl, or your baby has been gnawing on a piece of food, that food (and any communal portion it touched) should be thrown out rather than stored for later — saliva introduces bacteria that isn't accounted for in these storage windows.
- **When in doubt, throw it out.** These windows are intentionally conservative. If food has been sitting out longer than the room-temperature window, or you're not sure how long something's actually been in the fridge, it's not worth the risk.

## Storage windows by category

| Category | Fridge | Freezer | Room temperature | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fruit & vegetable purees | 48 hours | 2–3 months | 2 hours | Freeze in small, single-serving portions (ice cube trays work well) for easy thawing |
| Meat & fish purees / cooked meat or fish | 24 hours | 1–2 months | 2 hours | Shorter fridge window than produce — animal proteins spoil faster; cook thoroughly before storing |
| Cooked rice | 24 hours | 1 month | 1 hour | Cool rice quickly after cooking; reheat only once and serve steaming hot — cooked rice carries a higher bacterial risk if left at room temperature |
| Cooked grains (oats, quinoa, pasta) | 2–3 days | 1–2 months | 2 hours | Similar handling to rice; portion before freezing |
| Legumes (lentils, chickpeas, beans, tofu) | 3 days | 2–3 months | 2 hours | Cooked from dried or drained/rinsed from canned before storing |
| Dairy (yogurt, cheese, cooked egg dishes) | Follow package date for yogurt/cheese; cooked egg dishes 3 days | Not recommended for most dairy textures | 2 hours | Cooked egg (omelets, muffins) freezes reasonably well; yogurt and soft cheese do not freeze well texturally |
| Opened store-bought pouches | 24–48 hours, tightly sealed | Not recommended | 2 hours | **Never refreeze** a pouch once opened, even if it was previously frozen; discard if it's been open longer than 48 hours |
| Baked goods (muffins, pancakes, fritters) | 3 days | 1–2 months | 2 hours | Freeze well individually wrapped; thaw and reheat in small portions |

*These windows are intentionally conservative, prioritizing safety margin over squeezing out every last usable day. If a specific recipe calls for a different window (for example, an ingredient that spoils faster than its general category), follow the recipe's guidance instead.*

## Room-temperature limits — the 2-hour rule

Prepared food (and thawed food) shouldn't sit out at room temperature for more than about **2 hours** — or about **1 hour** in a hot room (above roughly 90°F/32°C), since bacteria multiply faster in warm conditions. This is the general food-safety "danger zone" rule, and it applies just as much to a bowl of baby food left out on the counter as it does to any other prepared food. If a meal has been sitting out longer than this window, don't try to save it for later — and definitely don't serve it.

## Moving food between fridge, freezer, and counter

If you move a prepared item from the freezer to the fridge to thaw, treat the fridge-storage clock as starting once it's thawed, not from the original freeze date. If you're tracking a pantry item in this app and change its storage location, the expiry countdown recalculates automatically based on the new location's window.

## A practical batch-cooking rhythm

Many parents find a weekly rhythm works well: batch-cook a couple of iron-rich bases and vitamin-C-paired sides once or twice a week, portion immediately into baby-sized servings, freeze most of it, and keep only 2–3 days' worth in the fridge. This stays within the windows above without daily cooking, while always serving freshly reheated (not repeatedly reheated) food.
