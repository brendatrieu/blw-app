A handful of rules in baby-led weaning aren't really about preference or gentle guidance — they're firm lines backed by clear reasons. This article covers the ones worth memorizing.

## No honey, in any form, before 12 months

This is the strictest rule on this list, and it deserves to be understood, not just followed.

> **Never give honey — in any form — to a baby under 12 months old, including baked into bread, muffins, granola bars, or cooked into sauces.**

Honey can contain spores of *Clostridium botulinum*. In adults and older children, these spores pass through the gut harmlessly. In babies under 12 months, the gut hasn't yet developed the protective bacteria and acidity to keep those spores from germinating — and if they do, they can produce a toxin that causes **infant botulism**, a serious illness affecting the nervous system.

The critical detail many parents miss: **baking, cooking, or heating honey does not reliably destroy the spores.** A honey graham cracker, a granola bar with honey in the ingredient list, honey-sweetened bread, or a splash of honey stirred into oatmeal are all off the table before 12 months, exactly the same as a spoonful of raw honey. Always check ingredient labels — honey shows up in more packaged baked goods than you'd expect.

After 12 months, once a baby's gut microbiome has matured, honey becomes fine to introduce.

## No added salt — sodium under 400mg/day for under-12-months

Babies' kidneys aren't yet equipped to process high sodium loads the way adult kidneys are, so the guidance is to keep total sodium intake under roughly **400mg per day** for babies under 12 months. In practice, this means:

- Don't add salt during cooking or at the table
- Be cautious with store-bought sauces, broths, cured meats, cheeses, and many packaged snacks, which can carry surprising amounts of sodium — check labels
- Cook from whole ingredients where you can, so you control what goes in
- A little naturally-occurring sodium from foods like cheese or bread is fine in the context of a varied diet — the concern is *added* salt and heavily processed high-sodium foods, not trace amounts

This isn't about making food bland forever — herbs, spices (non-spicy ones), garlic, lemon, and other flavor-builders are all great ways to make baby's food genuinely tasty without reaching for the salt shaker.

## No added sugar before 24 months

Current guidance recommends avoiding **added sugars entirely** for children under 24 months — not just going easy on them. This includes sugar, honey (also off-limits for the botulism reason above), syrups, and sweetened products, whether added during cooking or already baked into a store-bought item.

Naturally occurring sugars in whole fruit are a different story and are completely fine — a mashed banana or soft pear brings natural sweetness along with fiber and nutrients. The concern is *added* sugar, which offers empty calories at exactly the stage when your baby's small stomach needs every bite to be nutrient-dense, and which can shape sweet-taste preferences early on.

## No juice before 12 months

Juice — even 100% fruit juice — isn't recommended before 12 months. It offers sugar and calories without the fiber of whole fruit, can displace breastmilk or formula (or later, water and whole foods) in a small stomach, and doesn't offer meaningful benefits over eating the fruit itself. After 12 months, if you do offer juice, keep portions small and dilute it, and whole fruit remains the better choice nutritionally.

## Cow's milk as a drink only from 12 months

Cow's milk shouldn't replace breastmilk or formula as the main drink before 12 months — it doesn't have the right balance of iron, vitamins, and fats for a baby's needs at that stage, and can be harder on young kidneys in large volumes. This is specifically about cow's milk **as a drink**. Dairy foods like yogurt and cheese are a different matter — full-fat, pasteurized yogurt and cheese are great early foods and are commonly introduced well before 12 months as part of solids (see our [allergen introduction guide](/safety/allergen-introduction), since dairy is a top-9 allergen). Once a baby turns 12 months, whole cow's milk can typically be introduced as a drink, alongside continued breastmilk or formula per your pediatrician's guidance.

## Why these rules are firm rather than "gentle guidelines"

Most of baby-led weaning is about building good habits gradually and gently — but honey, sodium limits, added sugar, juice timing, and milk-as-a-drink timing are different. They're tied to specific, well-understood physiological realities (an immature gut microbiome, immature kidneys, small stomach capacity, taste-preference formation) rather than general nutrition preference, which is why they're worth treating as hard rules rather than aspirational ones.

## A simple label-reading habit

Because honey and added sugar hide in packaged foods more than people expect, it's worth building the habit of scanning the ingredient list — not just the "sugar" line on a nutrition label — on any store-bought snack, bread, or cracker before it becomes a regular in your baby's rotation.
