Introducing allergens used to come with advice to delay and avoid. Current guidance has flipped that completely: **early, deliberate introduction of allergenic foods — starting around 6 months, alongside the start of solids — is now understood to help reduce the risk of developing a food allergy**, particularly for peanut and egg. The goal isn't to avoid allergens; it's to introduce them thoughtfully.

## The top 9 allergens

These nine foods account for the vast majority of food allergies:

1. **Milk** (dairy)
2. **Egg**
3. **Peanut**
4. **Tree nut** (almond, cashew, walnut, etc.)
5. **Fish**
6. **Shellfish**
7. **Wheat**
8. **Soy**
9. **Sesame**

Every baby will encounter all nine eventually — the question is just how to sequence and manage that first exposure to each one safely.

## A sensible ladder order

There's no single mandatory order, but a common, sensible sequence — and the one this app defaults to — is:

**Egg → Peanut → Dairy → Wheat → Soy → Sesame → Tree nut → Fish → Shellfish**

The logic behind this order: egg and peanut are introduced early because they're both common allergens with strong evidence that early introduction reduces allergy risk, and both have easy, low-choking-risk starter forms (well-cooked scrambled egg; thinned, smooth peanut butter). The remaining allergens follow at a comfortable pace once you and your baby have a rhythm going. You don't need to rush — spreading the full ladder over several weeks to a couple of months is completely normal, and there's no harm in going slower.

## The one-at-a-time protocol

Whichever order you choose, the introduction protocol for *each* allergen is the same:

- **One new allergen at a time.** Don't introduce two allergens your baby has never had on the same day — if a reaction happens, you want to know exactly which food caused it.
- **Morning, at home.** Offer the new allergen earlier in the day rather than at dinner or before bed. This gives you a full day of awake, watchful hours to see how your baby responds, rather than trying to monitor for a reaction while everyone's asleep.
- **A tiny portion first.** Start with a very small amount — for example, a small taste of thinned peanut butter or a small piece of well-cooked scrambled egg — not a full serving. You're testing tolerance, not trying to fill your baby up on the first try.
- **Wait about 3 days before introducing the *next new* allergen.** This waiting window (commonly 3 days, sometimes extended to about 5 in more cautious plans) gives you a clear runway to notice both immediate reactions and the more delayed FPIES-type pattern (see our [allergic reaction signs guide](/safety/allergic-reaction-signs)) before adding another unknown into the mix. You can keep serving foods your baby has already tolerated during this window — the pause is specifically about *new* allergens.
- **Keep it in rotation once tolerated.** After a food is introduced and tolerated, keep serving it regularly — roughly **2 to 3 times per week** — rather than a one-and-done taste. Regular ongoing exposure is part of what helps build and maintain tolerance; a single taste followed by months of nothing doesn't offer the same benefit.

## Starter forms that keep choking risk low

Part of what makes early allergen introduction safe is choosing forms that are low-risk for choking as well as effective for exposure:

- **Egg** — well-cooked (no runny yolk), mashed or cut into soft strips
- **Peanut** — smooth peanut butter thinned with warm water or breastmilk/formula until runny, never a thick glob or whole/chopped nuts
- **Dairy** — full-fat plain yogurt or cheese, not cow's milk as a drink before 12 months (see our [honey, salt, and sugar guide](/safety/honey-salt-sugar))
- **Wheat** — soft toast strips, well-cooked pasta
- **Soy** — tofu, cut into soft strips or cubes
- **Sesame** — tahini, thinned and stirred into another food, similar to peanut butter
- **Tree nut** — smooth nut butter, thinned the same way as peanut butter
- **Fish** — well-cooked, flaked, and checked carefully for bones
- **Shellfish** — well-cooked, finely chopped or shredded

## If your baby has eczema or a known allergy already

Babies with moderate-to-severe eczema, or with an already-known food allergy, are at higher risk for developing additional food allergies. **Talk to your pediatrician or an allergist before starting allergen introduction** if this applies to your baby — they may recommend introducing peanut specifically under medical supervision, sometimes with an in-office first dose or allergy testing beforehand. This isn't a reason to skip allergen introduction altogether — early introduction still matters for these babies — it's a reason to bring in extra support before you start.

## Tracking makes this easier

Logging each allergen exposure — what, when, tiny taste vs. regular serving, and any reaction — is genuinely useful here, both so you can see your ladder progress at a glance and because a clean record is exactly what helps if you ever need to reason through a possible reaction later.

*This article is educational information, not medical advice. If your baby has eczema, a known food allergy, or any other risk factor, or if you have questions about allergen introduction timing, talk to your pediatrician or an allergist first — when in doubt, always check with your pediatrician.*
