Introducing new foods means occasionally watching for a reaction — and most of the time, what you'll see (if anything) is mild and manageable at home. But it's worth knowing the full spectrum clearly ahead of time, so you're not trying to look it up in the moment.

## Mild reactions

These are the most common kind, and they generally don't require anything more than watching, comforting your baby, and mentioning it to your pediatrician at the next visit (or sooner if it keeps happening with the same food).

**Mild signs to watch for:**

- A few hives or a small rash, especially around the mouth or chin
- Mild redness or flushing around the lips
- A little itching (baby rubbing their face or mouth)
- Mild stomach upset — a bit of fussiness, spit-up, or a looser stool
- Mild, brief coughing right after a bite touches the lips

Mild skin reactions right around the mouth are extremely common with certain foods (citrus, tomato, strawberry) simply because those foods irritate skin on contact — that's different from a true allergy and usually resolves with a quick wipe. A true allergic reaction tends to appear away from the contact area too, often alongside other signs on this list.

If you see mild signs, stop the meal, clean up, and keep a close eye on your baby for the next hour or two. Note what happened in your log so you and your pediatrician have a clear record before trying that food again.

## Emergency signs — call now

Some reactions escalate quickly and need immediate emergency care. This is not a "wait and see" list.

> **Call your local emergency number immediately if your baby shows any of the following after eating:**
> - Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or a whistling sound when breathing
> - Swelling of the lips, tongue, throat, or face
> - Persistent coughing, hoarseness, or a change in the sound of crying
> - Drooling or difficulty swallowing
> - Pale, blue, or gray skin color, especially around the lips
> - Sudden extreme sleepiness, limpness, or unresponsiveness
> - Widespread hives or swelling combined with any of the signs above (a reaction affecting the skin **and** a second body system — breathing, gut, or circulation — is a hallmark of anaphylaxis)
> - Repeated vomiting combined with lethargy or paleness (see the FPIES pattern below)
>
> If your baby has been prescribed emergency allergy medication (such as an epinephrine auto-injector), use it per your allergist's instructions and call emergency services — do not wait to see if symptoms improve first.

Reactions like this can develop within minutes of exposure, though timing varies. If you're debating whether something is "bad enough" to call — call. Responders would always rather field a call that turns out mild than miss one that wasn't.

## The FPIES pattern — a different kind of emergency

Food Protein-Induced Enterocolitis Syndrome (FPIES) is a delayed, non-IgE food reaction that looks completely different from the classic hives-and-swelling picture above, which is exactly what makes it easy to miss. It doesn't show up on typical allergy testing, and it doesn't usually involve hives at all.

**The FPIES pattern to recognize:**

- **Repetitive, forceful vomiting** starting roughly **1 to 4 hours** after eating the trigger food (this delayed timing, rather than immediate reaction, is the key distinguishing feature)
- Vomiting that keeps going, rather than a single spit-up
- **Lethargy** — baby becomes unusually floppy, listless, or hard to rouse
- **Pallor** — skin looks pale or washed out
- Sometimes followed by diarrhea a few hours later
- In more severe episodes, dehydration and even a drop in blood pressure can follow

Common trigger foods include milk, soy, rice, oats, and egg, though any food can potentially trigger it. Because the reaction is delayed and vomiting can become severe, an FPIES episode with lethargy or pallor should be treated as an emergency the same way anaphylaxis is — it just looks different and shows up later.

> **If your baby has repetitive vomiting 1–4 hours after a new food, combined with lethargy or pallor, call your local emergency number or go to emergency care — this pattern needs urgent evaluation even without hives or breathing symptoms.**

## Why the allergen introduction protocol matters here

This is exactly why we recommend introducing new allergens one at a time, in the morning, at home — see our [allergen introduction guide](/safety/allergen-introduction). A single-new-food morning makes a trigger easier to identify, and gives you awake, watchful daytime hours during the window when both immediate and delayed reactions are most likely to appear.

## What to do after any reaction

Whether the reaction was mild or serious, log it — food, amount, timing, and exactly what you observed — in your baby's profile. This record is genuinely useful both for your pediatrician and for helping you and any AI features in this app reason accurately about patterns over time. Don't reintroduce a suspected trigger food until you've talked to your pediatrician, even if the reaction seemed mild.
