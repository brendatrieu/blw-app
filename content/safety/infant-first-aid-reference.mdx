> **This article is a reminder, not a training course. It cannot teach you infant CPR or choking first aid safely on its own. Please take a certified infant first-aid and CPR course before you need this information for real — see the note at the end of this article.**

The single best thing you can do to prepare for a choking or breathing emergency is get certified, hands-on training before it happens. Reading steps in an emergency, or trying to recall a half-remembered article, is a poor substitute for muscle memory built through practice on a training mannequin under an instructor's guidance. This article exists purely as a general orientation and a reminder for parents who have already trained — not as a way to learn the technique for the first time.

## First: recognize the emergency

As covered in our [gagging vs. choking guide](/safety/gagging-vs-choking), the key sign of true choking is **silence** — no crying, no coughing, no sound — sometimes with a weak or absent cough, difficulty breathing, or blue/dusky skin color.

> **If your baby cannot cough, cry, or breathe, call your local emergency number (or have someone else call while you begin first aid) immediately. Do not wait to see if it resolves.**

If your baby is coughing forcefully, crying, or making sound, they are likely still moving air — this usually means you should stay close, let them try to clear it themselves, and avoid interfering, since back blows or attempting to remove something you can see can sometimes push it further in.

## General shape of infant choking first aid

Certified courses teach the specific hand positions, force, and rhythm in detail — the general shape of the response, for a conscious infant under 1 year who cannot cough, cry, or breathe, typically involves:

1. **Call for emergency help** — yourself, or immediately direct someone nearby to call while you start first aid.
2. **A sequence of back blows** — with the infant face-down along your forearm, supported securely, head lower than the chest, delivered firmly with the heel of your hand between the shoulder blades.
3. **A sequence of chest thrusts** — with the infant turned face-up along your other forearm, head still lower than the chest, using two fingers on the center of the breastbone, distinct from adult abdominal thrusts (abdominal thrusts are not used on infants).
4. **Alternating between these sequences**, checking the mouth for a visible object between rounds (only removing something you can actually see and reach — never a blind finger sweep), and continuing until the object is dislodged, emergency help arrives, or the infant becomes unresponsive.
5. **If the infant becomes unresponsive**, first-aid guidance shifts to infant CPR, which is a distinct skill set with its own technique for compressions and rescue breaths sized appropriately for an infant.

This is intentionally a general outline, not step-by-step instructions — the exact hand placement, force, and count are precisely what a certified course will drill into you safely, on a mannequin, with feedback from an instructor. Doing it wrong in practice is low-stakes; doing it wrong for the first time during a real emergency is not.

## While waiting for emergency services generally

Beyond active choking response, a few general reminders for any infant emergency while help is on the way:

- **Stay on the line** with emergency services if you called — they can often talk you through steps in real time and will tell you what to do next.
- **Keep your baby as calm as possible** and avoid unnecessary movement if there's any concern about injury.
- **Have your address and key information ready** to give quickly — this is worth knowing cold before an emergency, not looking up during one.
- **If your baby loses consciousness and stops breathing normally**, be ready to begin infant CPR if you're trained, and continue until help arrives or the baby recovers.
- **After any choking incident**, even one that resolved on its own, mention it to your pediatrician — sometimes a partial obstruction or aspiration needs a follow-up check even after the immediate danger has passed.

## Please take a certified course

We say this as strongly as we can: **take a certified infant and child first-aid / CPR course**, ideally before you start solid foods, and refresh your training periodically (many certifications recommend renewal every one to two years). These courses are typically a few hours, widely available through hospitals, the Red Cross, the American Heart Association, and local community health organizations, and often specifically cover the infant-specific techniques that differ from adult or even child first aid. Many parents find it genuinely reassuring, not just useful — knowing you've practiced the actual motions removes a layer of anxiety from everyday mealtimes.

This article — and this app generally — is meant to complement that training with context and reminders, never to replace it.
